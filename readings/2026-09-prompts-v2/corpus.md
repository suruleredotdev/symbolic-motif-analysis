# Kings, war and the diviner's world: a town's carvings, read as a record

## 1. The story in brief

Most of these carvings were recorded in 1910 at Modákẹ́kẹ́, beside Ilé-Ifẹ̀. Oyo refugees founded the town around 1845. It fought Ifẹ̀ for decades, sided with Ibadan in the Kiriji war, and in 1909 was emptied under the peace terms of 1886. Frobenius's own captions say a carved temple board was "excavated" there in 1910 (KBA_19153–19155). Read in order, the collection tells three linked stories. The first is **who ruled**: lines of crowned figures holding two staffs, the same king image carved at Ifẹ̀ and at Modákẹ́kẹ́. The second is **how power was won in the nineteenth-century wars**: a palace door from Ado-Ekiti runs from king to army to kneeling subjects to roped captives, and rifles appear even on Modákẹ́kẹ́'s divining trays. The third is **how people lived with that world**: Ifá trays whose rims set out what a client brings to the diviner (marriage, children, danger) and the offering that settles it, all under the face of Ẹ̀ṣù, the messenger. This is not one chronicle. It is closer to a town's archive of kingship, war and the means of asking fate, gathered as the town ended.

## 2. Reading the collection in order

### Rulers: the Ifẹ̀ planks and the Modákẹ́kẹ́ temple board

The clearest statement of rule is on two carved planks from Ifẹ̀. **EBA-Div_00311_panel_01** and **EBA-Div_00312_panel_01** each show three crowned figures holding staffs, stacked one above another inside a woven frame. On both, the middle figure's legs curl outward like loops, the "mudfish-legged" king known from Ifẹ̀ and Benin art and linked to Olókun's sea wealth. Six crowned figures in two matching columns come closer to a king list than anything else in the collection. The companion planks add a ranked staff-holder with kneeling attendants and a pierced spiral (**EBA-Div_00311_panel_00**), and a lizard or crocodile seen from above beside fringed regalia (**EBA-Div_00311_panel_02**). The old reading took that lizard for a person.

The same king image appears at Modákẹ́kẹ́. Three details copied from one temple board (Frobenius 1923, plate 184) show:

- a figure with a tall ridged crown, two staffs and looped legs (**KBA_19154**);
- a bearded figure gripping the frame, whose legs sweep back to it (**KBA_19153**);
- an armed figure with blades crossed over its chest (**KBA_19155**).

The two rival towns shared one picture of kingship, and one board held both the king and the war leader.

*How it builds:* a crown, two staffs and looped legs make a king; a king repeated in a frame makes a succession; the same king type in two rival towns makes a shared idea of authority.

### War: the Ado-Ekiti door

**EBA-Div_00303**, the palace door of the Ewì of Ado-Ekiti, is the one object built as a war story. Read from top to bottom:

1. The crowned king sits with attendants, beside a beaded crown and a grid of studs.
2. Next comes the army: archers with quivers, horses and guards.
3. Lines of six and seven people kneel in submission.
4. Two leaders are marked by crossed bands and pointed, triangle-framed heads, with a monkey nearby.
5. Riflemen follow in files of six and seven.
6. Last, captives roped at the neck carry headloads under a rifle guard.

The rifles and the roped files are period evidence: in the Kiriji/Ekitiparapọ war (1877–86) Ado-Ekiti fought with the Ekiti–Ijesa confederacy against Ibadan and Modákẹ́kẹ́. The door does not show one battle. It records the kingdom's strength, from king to captives.

The leaders' marks, **crossed bands** and a **pointed hood**, recur elsewhere:

- on a door Frobenius called "mythological" (**KBA_19157**);
- on the armed figure of the Modákẹ́kẹ́ board (**KBA_19155**);
- on the tray face of **FoA_04-5583**;
- on the hooded Ẹ̀ṣù face at the head of the 48 cm tray **KBA_17556**, copied separately on **KBA_17553**.

The same mark on war leaders and on Ẹ̀ṣù suggests one sign for a person who stands at a crossing and commands. Frobenius's "mythology" label on KBA_19157 may hide a war chief.

### The diviner's world: trays, tappers and vessels

Twenty of the 37 items are divination instruments. Every tray is headed by a face, the *ojú ọpọ́n* ("face of the tray"), Ẹ̀ṣù who carries the offering. Around it the rims fall into a few recurring programmes:

- **The client's world.** **EBA-Div_00302** and **FoA_04-5580** repeat each other motif for motif: couples, a kneeling rifleman, a fowl for sacrifice, lidded faces (5580 adds a nursing mother). They very probably come from one Modákẹ́kẹ́ workshop. The owner's own labels on 00302, a bound couple and a kneeling rifleman, set the key: marriage, children and safety, in a time of guns.
- **Creation.** **FoA_04-5583** and its drawn twin **KBA_10305** set mirrored chameleons with coiled tails beside the face. This may quote the Ifẹ̀ story in which a hen spread the first earth and a chameleon tested it.
- **Offering and danger.** Different rims show trussed animals (**FoA_04-5585**), scorpions, a crocodile and a tortoise (**FoA_04-5586**), crocodiles, fish and tortoises (**KBA_10300**), and a crab, mudfish and two fowls (**FoA_04-5589**). **KBA_10298** has a serpent knotted in every compartment. These are the powers of water and bush that an offering keeps in balance.
- **Community.** **FoA_04-5588**, also drawn as **KBA_10299**, crowds its rim with drummers, carriers, fighters and fallen bodies. **FoA_04-5947** reads like an inventory of town life: weapons, a drum, a game board, medicinal leaves, a crocodile and tusks.
- **Pattern.** Four trays are made mostly of plaits and geometric units:
  - **FoA_04-5587** (varied plaits);
  - **KBA_15356** (nested squares and a triangle chain);
  - **KBA_17555** (twelve identical fish-head units, perhaps the *agbérí ẹja* pattern shared with cloth);
  - **KBA_17556** (blocks of cowries or kola, and medicine gourds).

The rest of the kit completes the picture:

- the ìróké tappers that call Ifá (**FoA_04-5598**, **FoA_04-5599**), each with a kneeling devotee;
- a staff with a woman offering a bowl (**FoA_04-5597**);
- a lidded vessel guarded by a coiled serpent (**KBA_10274**);
- Ẹ̀ṣù himself, with his long cap and pipe, copied from a carved door (**KBA_17557**).

### Binding: posts and plait

The Modákẹ́kẹ́ posts (**FoA_04-5578**) frame figures in endless plait: one figure with raised hands, and a pair of lizard-figures sharing a lizard's spine and head. Plait binds almost every object here, from door frames to tray rims. Aronson's warning about Yoruba *aṣọ olona* applies: the meaning of a woven pattern is "far from watertight". The same interlace can mean differently on cloth, wood and in speech, so its names should be taken from users, not assumed.

**From motif to story.** A crown, a rifle or a fowl is a word. An object arranges the words into a statement: *this king*, *this war*, *this client's trouble and its offering*. The collection, gathered at one town in the year after it was emptied, sets those statements side by side: the rulers who claimed the land, the wars that decided it, and the means people used to steer through both.

## 4. What this says about the thesis

**Panels as chronicles: supported, in two forms.**

- **A king list.** The Ifẹ̀ planks give a king list in outline: six crowned figures in two columns, with a king type shared with Modákẹ́kẹ́. Nothing on them names a ruler.
- **A war chronicle.** The Ado-Ekiti door is a true war chronicle, ordered from king to army to captives and dated by its rifles.

The trays are instruments, not chronicles, but they carry the period with them: the rifle on a Modákẹ́kẹ́ divining tray is history as well as symbol. The serpents of renewal are present, but a true ouroboros is not.

**Pattern as number: open.** The best candidates are:

- the counted files on the Ado door (four archers; kneeling lines of six and seven; rifle files of six and seven);
- the twelve rim units of KBA_17555;
- the geometric sides of KBA_15356;
- the plait segments of FoA_04-5587;
- the eight ribbed blocks of FoA_04-5589.

None of them yet matches an Ifá count (16 or 256) or a reign count. They need to be counted on the objects and tested against oral lists.

**What kind of record.** The collection mixes four kinds of record:

- **emblems**: the king image;
- **a chronicle**: the war door;
- **instruments**: trays and tappers;
- **inventories**: FoA_04-5947.

James C. Scott's point fits: a town at war kept its memory in carving and performance rather than on paper, and this collection holds a piece of that memory, taken as the town was emptied.

## 5. Where to check it

- **Ifẹ̀ king list and palace tradition.** The oríkì of the Ọọ̀ni and the palace's own account of the king list, to test whether the crowned columns (00311_01, 00312_01) name anyone. Compare the looped-leg king with the documented Ifẹ̀ and Benin mudfish-legged figures.
- **Ado-Ekiti.** The Ewì's oríkì and Ekiti war histories (Samuel Johnson's *History of the Yorubas*; S. A. Akintoye's *Revolution and Power Politics in Yorubaland*) for the door's army and captives, and for who wore crossed bands.
- **Modákẹ́kẹ́.** Town histories and elders' accounts of the 1909 evacuation, and of which shrines and compounds held the temple board and trays.
- **Frobenius.** His field notes and his books (*Und Afrika sprach*, 1912; *Das unbekannte Afrika*, 1923, plates 183–184) for exact findspots and what "excavated" meant. The German museums that received the D.I.A.F.E. objects may still hold the carvings themselves.
- **Ifá.** Ifá verses and diviners' own readings of tray rims, for the chameleon and creation story, the fowl and the counted units (Bascom's *Ifa Divination*, 1969, for tray layout). On whether woven patterns carry fixed meanings, see Aronson on *aṣọ olona*.

**Data notes**

1. Many detections cover a whole side of a tray or a whole mount sheet rather than one motif, and some key figures (the head of KBA_17556, the foot of KBA_10300) are not detected at all.
2. 14 of the 37 items are drawings or mount sheets. Three repeat other items (KBA_10299 = FoA_04-5588; KBA_10305 ≈ FoA_04-5583; KBA_17553 copies KBA_17556), so they are not independent evidence.
3. The counts cited above (6, 7, 12, 8) were read from the images, not measured; check them on the objects.
4. Only two motif labels, both on EBA-Div_00302, come from a person. Every other label is generated and provisional.
